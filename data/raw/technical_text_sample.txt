Information theory studies the quantification, storage, and communication of information. Originally proposed by Claude Shannon in 1948, the fundamental limits of data compression and reliable transmission are governed by entropy and channel capacity.

Entropy quantifies the average uncertainty associated with a random source symbol. In lossless data compression, the theoretical limit of average codeword length is strictly lower-bounded by the source entropy H(X). Coding techniques such as range Asymmetric Numeral Systems approach this fundamental bound with high operational throughput by representing symbol streams on continuous probability intervals.

Channel capacity defines the supremum of mutual information between the transmitter and receiver over all possible input distributions. According to Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem, reliable communication with arbitrarily small probability of error is achievable at any transmission rate below the channel capacity C. For a binary symmetric channel with crossover probability p, the channel capacity is given by C = 1 - H_2(p), where H_2 is the binary entropy function. Polar codes, invented by Erdal Arikan, achieve this symmetric capacity asymptotically through channel polarization.
